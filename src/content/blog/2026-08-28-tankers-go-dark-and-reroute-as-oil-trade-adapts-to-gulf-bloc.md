Oil traders are using creative workarounds to keep crude flowing out of the Gulf despite the Strait of Hormuz being effectively choked off. Smaller shuttle tankers load crude inside the Gulf, thread through Hormuz, and transfer their cargo ship-to-ship once clear of the strait to split up shipments. Additionally, some tankers have been switching off their tracking transponders when leaving the strait to mask their movements.

On the other side of the Arabian Peninsula, Saudi Arabia has shifted crude toward the Suez Canal because Houthi forces effectively closed the Bab al-Mandeb route with a declared maritime embargo and strikes on export infrastructure. Because the canal is too shallow for fully loaded supertankers, exporters are forced to use smaller Suezmax vessels. This doubles the ships needed for the same volume, adds roughly $2 million in extra charter costs per voyage, and adds about a month to transit times compared to the direct route.

Together, these methods form an improvised, more expensive shadow logistics network that keeps Gulf oil reaching global markets while the region's two main chokepoints remain contested. These extra costs and delivery times are already appearing in freight rates and delivery schedules, with traders reporting no clear end in sight.
