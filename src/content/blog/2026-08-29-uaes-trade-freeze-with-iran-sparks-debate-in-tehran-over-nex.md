The United Arab Emirates has halted trade and financial dealings with Iran, beating the Trump administration to the punch. This move has sparked a debate inside Tehran among officials about their next steps, even though some suspect the Emiratis will not follow through completely. 

The stakes are high because the UAE is Iran's largest market for fuel oil, taking nearly 70% of those exports. For years, Dubai has also served as Iran's main re-export hub and financial workaround for international banking networks blocked by direct sanctions. Losing this relationship, even partially, threatens about $14.8 billion in annual trade and removes a durable channel that survived tighter Western sanctions.

Majidreza Hariri, head of the Iran-China Joint Chamber of Commerce, is publicly weighing alternatives such as Oman, Iraq, or Turkey. However, this debate reflects anxiety rather than confidence, as none of these alternatives match Dubai's decades-old infrastructure, banking relationships, and logistics networks. The pressure is further compounded because Iran's central bank governor recently confirmed that oil export revenue has effectively fallen to zero, while a 60-day diplomatic window for a settlement has expired without resolution.
