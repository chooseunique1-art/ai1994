At least 19 people have died in Nagaland over the past two days after consuming suspected spurious liquor, with eight deaths recorded in Mokokchung district and eleven in Tuensang district, according to state authorities.  Officials say samples of the seized alcohol have been sent to a forensic laboratory in Guwahati to determine whether methanol contamination or another toxic substance was responsible for the deaths, since preliminary symptoms reported by victims, including dizziness, blurred vision and breathing difficulty, are consistent with methanol poisoning from illicitly distilled or diluted liquor.  Nagaland has enforced statewide prohibition since 1990, but a thriving illegal liquor trade has persisted despite the ban, periodically resulting in fatal poisoning incidents when adulterated batches reach consumers.  In response to this latest tragedy, police have arrested 19 people connected to the alleged supply chain and seized close to 3,000 bottles of liquor in a series of raids across the affected districts.

The state government has formed a Special Investigation Team, headed by an Additional Superintendent of Police, with a mandate to establish exactly how the tainted liquor was produced, distributed and sold, and to identify anyone further up the supply chain who has so far evaded arrest.  Authorities have issued a public health advisory urging residents not to consume any unlabelled or unidentified alcohol and describing the warning signs of poisoning so that people showing early symptoms can seek emergency treatment before the condition becomes fatal.  Investigators have said they have already obtained some leads in tracing the source of the liquor but consider it too early to disclose further details publicly while the inquiry is ongoing.  The scale of the death toll, and the fact that it stems from two separate but seemingly connected clusters of poisoning within a matter of days, has raised fresh questions about the effectiveness of enforcement under the state's decades-old prohibition regime.

Nagaland has recorded similar hooch-related tragedies in the past, and this incident is likely to renew debate over whether prohibition itself inadvertently drives consumers toward unregulated and more dangerous sources of alcohol.  Local hospitals in the affected districts have been placed on alert for further cases, since the delayed onset of methanol poisoning symptoms means additional victims could still emerge from the same contaminated batches even as the immediate death toll appears to have stabilised.  The state government now faces pressure to show that the promised supply-chain investigation produces concrete accountability rather than only the arrests of low-level sellers already in custody.
